Finding a place

What to ask a flatmate before you say yes

A room is easy to judge in ten minutes. The person in the next room takes longer. Half an hour of honest questions now saves a year of notes on the fridge.

The short version

  • Ask about ordinary days, not personalities: sleep, cleaning, food, guests, noise, money.
  • Answer every question yourself first. It is a conversation, not an interview.
  • "I'm fine with anything" is not an answer. Ask for the last time it came up.
  • Agree the money, and what happens if someone leaves, before anyone pays a deposit.

Nobody falls out with a flatmate over world views. It is the alarm that goes off six times at 5.30, the pan that "is soaking", the friend who stayed for a week. Small things, every day, with no door you can close on them.

The good news is that these are easy to find out in advance. You only have to ask, and to answer honestly yourself.

Ask about ordinary days

Skip "are you clean?" and "are you chill?". Everybody is, in their own telling. Ask what a normal Tuesday looks like instead, and listen for where it rubs against yours.

Sleep and work

  • When do you usually sleep, and when are you up?
  • Do you work from home? On calls a lot? Any night shifts?
  • Are you a light sleeper? Does a TV in the hall at midnight bother you?

A night owl and an early bird can share a flat happily. A night owl who takes calls in the hall and an early bird with a thin door cannot.

Cleaning

  • How often should the kitchen and the bathroom be cleaned, and by whom?
  • Dishes: the same day, or when the sink is full?
  • Is there help at home? Who pays, and who deals with it when she doesn't turn up?

"Clean" is the word people mean most differently. Get to specifics. Once a week or once a day is a real difference; neither is wrong.

Food and the kitchen

  • Do you cook? Is there a cook? Shared groceries, or a shelf each?
  • Any rules for the kitchen, such as vegetarian only, or no eggs in the shared pans?
  • Is the fridge shared? What about the last of the milk?

Kitchen rules matter a great deal to some people and not at all to others. If they matter to you, say so at the start. It is far kinder than discovering it over someone's dinner.

Guests

  • How often do you have friends over? On weeknights?
  • Overnight guests: fine, fine with a heads-up, or not at all?
  • A partner who stays often: how often is often?

Noise, smoke and drinks

  • Music and TV: how loud, and until when?
  • Do you smoke? Indoors, on the balcony, or not at home?
  • Drinking at home: sometimes, every weekend, never?

Money

  • How is the rent split, and who pays the owner?
  • Which bills are there, and how are they shared?
  • Whose name is on the agreement? Who paid the deposit, and how much is my share?
  • If one of us leaves early, who finds the next person, and who covers the gap?

Money questions feel rude and are the least rude thing you can ask. Splitting the rent fairly and what the agreement should say each get an article of their own.

Ask about the last time, not about what they would do. People describe their best selves; the last time is what happened.

Make it a conversation

Nobody enjoys being interviewed for a room. Three things make it feel like two people working something out:

  1. Go first. Say how you live before you ask how they do. "I'm usually asleep by eleven and I can't sleep through a TV. What about you?"
  2. Ask about the last flat. "What worked in your last place? What drove you up the wall?" You learn what they notice, and how they talk about people they have lived with.
  3. Say the awkward thing now. Whatever you are hoping won't come up will come up, in month two, at a worse moment.

Instead of

Are you okay with guests?

Try

My sister visits about once a month and stays the weekend. Would that work for you?

Answers that should make you pause

None of these means a bad person. Each of them means a question you should ask twice.

  • "I'm easy, anything is fine." To everything. Either they have not thought about it or they are saying what gets them the room. Ask for an example.
  • Vague about money. Who pays the owner, what the deposit was, what the bills come to. If these are hazy before you move in, they will not get clearer after.
  • Every past flatmate was a nightmare. One bad flatmate is bad luck. Five is a pattern, and you are about to be number six.
  • A hurry. "Someone else is coming at six, pay a token now." A good flat can be gone by evening, that part is true. But nobody who wants a flatmate they will get along with refuses you a day to think.
  • Nothing in writing. Not the split, not the deposit, not your name on the agreement.
