Rental scams: the red flags, and what to do if you paid

A flat that is perfect, cheap and about to be taken by someone else. That is not luck, it is a script. Here is how the common rental scams work, how to spot one, and what to do in the first hour if you have already paid.

The short version

  • No visit, no money. Every common rental scam needs you to pay before you have stood inside the flat.
  • A hurry is the tell: "someone else is paying this evening" is how the script starts.
  • You never scan a QR code or enter your UPI PIN to receive money. Ever.
  • Pay only to the owner's own account, and get what it is for in writing.
  • If you have paid, call 1930 at once and file on cybercrime.gov.in. Minutes matter.

Flat-hunting makes sensible people rush. You are new to the city, the good places really do go in a day, and you have already seen four that were awful. That is exactly the state of mind a rental scam is written for.

The scams themselves are simple, and they repeat. Once you know the five scripts, you will recognise the opening line.

How the common ones work

1. "Pay a token to block it"

A flat is posted on a marketplace, a social-media group or a messaging channel. The "owner" is friendly and quick to reply. There is one problem: he is out of town, or abroad, so you can't see it today. But another tenant is ready to pay this evening, so if you want it, send a booking amount now.

Maharashtra's cyber police issued an advisory about exactly this in November 2025: fraudsters posing as owners or agents, no physical visit, pressure to pay to "block" the flat, contact only on chat apps.1 In a case reported from Bengaluru, a 27-year-old paid booking charges, a deposit and "processing fees" to "owners" he met in a flat-hunting group on a messaging app, lost ₹6.25 lakh, and was blocked.2

2. The flat that is too good

Lovely photos, a great address, rent well under what the area costs. The pictures may be lifted from another listing, or not be of a real flat at all: Bengaluru police have said scammers were using AI-generated images of luxury flats.3 The low rent is not a bargain. It is the bait that makes you hurry.

3. The fee before the visit

A "visitor pass", a "registration charge", a "processing fee", usually small enough to pay without thinking. In the Bengaluru cases, one house-hunter was asked for ₹2,000 as a visitor-pass fee and then for an advance.3 Owners don't charge you to look at their flat.

4. The owner who isn't

Someone shows you a real flat, takes a real deposit, signs a real-looking agreement, and doesn't own it. In one Bengaluru case, tenants found out only when the housing society came asking for dues.4 This one is the hardest to spot, because you did see the flat. It is why the ownership check below matters.

5. If you are the one with a room: the officer who pays in advance

This one targets people listing a room. The caller says he is in the army, or another uniformed service, and has been posted to your city. He sends ID cards. He doesn't need to see the flat. He will pay the deposit today, but his department's "rules" mean you have to send a small amount first, or scan a code, and it comes straight back.

It doesn't. The national cyber-crime centre has warned about fraudsters posing as army personnel to borrow the trust people have in the forces.5 A man in Hyderabad who listed his flat lost more than ₹12.75 lakh to a caller posing as a commando.6 A woman in Bengaluru who advertised her house was walked through a payment app to "receive" an advance and lost ₹98,000.7

You never scan a code or enter your PIN to receive money. Receiving needs nothing from you.

That line is not ours. The Reserve Bank's own booklet on frauds says there is no need to enter a PIN or password anywhere to receive money, and never to scan a QR code to receive it.8

The red flags, in one place

Any one of these means slow down. Two means walk away.

  • You can't see the flat , for any reason, however good the reason sounds.
  • A hurry. Another tenant, a deadline, a discount that ends tonight.
  • Rent far below the area's.
  • Chat only. No call, no video, no meeting. Or a call from a number that isn't the one in the listing.
  • ID cards sent as proof. A photo of an ID proves that someone has a photo of an ID.5
  • Any fee before a visit.
  • An agreement sent to you before you have seen anything , to make it feel official.1
  • The account isn't the owner's. A "cousin", an "accountant", a wallet number.
  • You are asked to scan, approve or enter a PIN to get money.

If you have already paid

Speed is everything. A payment can sometimes be stopped before it is moved on, and that window is short. By mid-2026, the national reporting system behind the 1930 helpline had stopped more than ₹11,000 crore from reaching fraudsters.9

  1. Call 1930, now. It is the national cyber-crime helpline. Tell them the amount, the time, and the account or UPI ID it went to. Don't wait to collect every detail first.
  2. File the complaint on cybercrime.gov.in the same day. The helpline call starts things; the portal makes it a complaint the police can act on.11
  3. Tell your bank at once. Use the app or its helpline, and ask them to raise a dispute. The longer you take to tell the bank, the worse your position.14 A payment you made yourself isn't guaranteed to come back, so treat this as a chance, not a promise.
  4. Keep everything. The transaction ID or UTR, the UPI ID or account number, screenshots of the chat, the listing's link, the phone numbers. Don't delete the chat in anger.
  5. Report the listing and the number. On the platform where you found it, and on Chakshu, the government's page for reporting suspected fraud calls and messages. Chakshu is for the message, not for the lost money: that is steps 1 and 2.12
If you ever feel unsafe

On a visit, with someone who turns up, or anywhere else: leave, and call 112. A deposit is only money.

It is not about being careless

People reported more than 65 lakh financial-fraud complaints on the national portal between 2021 and 2025, adding up to over ₹55,000 crore.10 These scripts work on doctors, engineers and bankers. They work because they are aimed at a moment, not at a kind of person: tired, in a new city, with a deadline.

The whole defence fits in one line. See it, check who owns it, then pay. In that order, however good the flat is.
