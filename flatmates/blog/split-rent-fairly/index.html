Split the rent without falling out

Equal isn't always fair, and "fair" is whatever the whole flat agrees to. Here is a simple way to get there, with a calculator that does the sums.

The short version

  • Agree the split before anyone picks a room. Afterwards, everyone argues for the room they already have.
  • Shared space is split by head. The rooms are split by what each one is worth.
  • The test of a fair split: at these prices, nobody wants to swap rooms.
  • Bills can follow a different rule from the rent. Decide the AC before the first summer bill.
  • Put the split, the deposit shares and what happens when someone leaves in writing.

Three people take a flat for ₹30,000. One room has its own bathroom and a balcony. One is a box by the kitchen. Ten thousand each?

It is the simplest answer, and in most flats somebody quietly resents it by the third month. An equal split can be a good deal for whoever has the biggest room, and a poor one for a couple squeezed into a single bedroom.1 Rooms are rarely the same, so it is fair to ask whether they should cost the same.

There is no official formula. There is a way of thinking about it that most flats can agree on.

Three ways to split the rent

1. Equally

Right when the rooms really are much alike, or when the difference is small enough that nobody cares. It has one big advantage: nobody has to do sums.

2. By room

The usual fix. Each room gets a price, and a better room costs more. What counts as better is up to the flat. Size and a bathroom of your own are the usual two.3 A balcony, light and air, and which room hears the road come up as well.

3. By what each room is worth to you

Here is a test from the mathematics of fair division. Put a price on every room. Then ask each person: at these prices, which room would you choose? If two people want the same room, it is too cheap; raise it and lower the others. Keep going until everyone picks a different room.

At that point nobody wants to swap their room and its price for anyone else's, which is about as good a definition of fair as you will find.4 The mathematician Francis Su proved that, given a few reasonable assumptions, such a set of prices always exists.5 You don't need the proof. You need ten minutes and a flat that is willing to say numbers out loud.

What about splitting by income?

Some friends and most couples do it. It can work well when there is a big difference in what people earn.1 Between flatmates who met last month, it rarely does: it turns the rent into a conversation about salaries.

A calculator to start from

This one uses the second way, with one idea added: the flat is partly shared space (the hall, the kitchen, the common bathroom), which everyone uses alike, and partly rooms, which they don't. So the share of the rent that pays for shared space is split by head, and the rest by room.

Rent split calculator

Half the rent pays for the space everyone uses and is split by head. The other half is split between the rooms: a large room counts for a fifth more than a medium one and a small room for a fifth less; a room with its own bathroom counts for 15% more, and a balcony for 5% more. Two people in one room each pay a head's share of the shared space and split the room between them.

The percentages it starts with are ours, not a rule: half the rent for shared space, a fifth up or down for a size, 15% for a bathroom of your own, 5% for a balcony. Open "Change how it is worked out" and move them until the numbers feel right to everyone. Then run the swap test: at these prices, would anyone rather have another room? If yes, keep adjusting.

A fair split is one nobody thinks about again.

Then the bills

Bills don't have to follow the rent. They can go by head, by room or by use, whichever fits the bill.2

The AC question

The flat has one meter, so the bill is for everyone's units together. In many states the price of a unit also rises as the month's total climbs, which means one room's AC can make everybody's units dearer. Your own bill shows the slabs it was charged in.

Three ways flats deal with it, from least fuss to most exact:

  • A flat add-on. Whoever runs an AC adds a fixed amount in the hot months. Easy, and rough.
  • Split the extra. Take a mild month's bill as the base. In summer, everyone splits the base by head and the AC rooms split whatever is above it.
  • Measure it. A small energy meter on the AC's socket, or the AC's own app if it has one, shows its units. Those go to the room; the rest is split by head.

Instead of

You run the AC all night. You should pay more.

Try

The bill jumps in summer. Shall we split the AC's units separately from April, so nobody has to keep score?

The deposit and the one-time costs

Split the deposit in the same proportion as the rent, and write down who paid what. When the flat is given up, the refund goes back in those shares, less any deductions the flat agrees are shared.

The same goes for anything bought together: the fridge, the washing machine, the router. Decide on the day you buy it who keeps it, or how it is bought out, when someone leaves. A second-hand fridge has ended more friendships than it deserves.

When someone leaves

It will happen, usually in the middle of a month and often in a hurry. Agree three things on day one:

  1. Notice between yourselves. Usually the same as the notice the owner wants, so nobody is caught out.
  2. Who pays for the empty room. The fair answer in most flats: the person leaving pays until their notice ends or a replacement moves in, whichever is first.
  3. The deposit share. A common way: the person moving in pays the leaver's share to the leaver, and steps into their place. Agree it in writing, and tell the owner whose name is changing.

Write it down

Not a contract. A message in the flat's group that everyone replies "agreed" to. Six months from now, nobody remembers what was said in the hall on moving day.
