A rent agreement is a short document that decides three things you will care about a great deal: how much you pay, how you can leave, and how much of your deposit you see again. It is worth twenty minutes.

Rules differ by state, so this is a map of what to look for, with the law it rests on linked at the end.

## Why it is always 11 months {#eleven-months}

Because of one line in a law from 1908. The Registration Act says a lease must be registered if it runs from year to year, or for any term of more than a year.[^1] Registering costs money and a visit or an online filing, so owners and tenants got into the habit of writing agreements for eleven months and renewing them.

Three things follow.

- **Eleven months is a habit, not a rule.** No law says a rental must be for eleven months.
- **An unregistered 11-month agreement is still an agreement.** Under the central law it did not need registering. One that did need it and wasn't registered is weak as evidence if things end up in court.[^1]
- **Stamp duty still applies.** Each state sets its own rate, and an agreement on plain paper is asking for trouble.

Some states ask for more than the central law does. Two that matter to a lot of renters:

### Maharashtra: registered, whatever the length

In Maharashtra, every leave and licence or tenancy agreement must be **in writing and registered**, even for eleven months. Registering it is **the owner's responsibility**. If there is no registered agreement, the law says the tenant's account of the terms stands unless the owner proves otherwise, and the owner can be fined or jailed for up to three months.[^2]

It can be done online, without going to the sub-registrar's office, through the state's e-registration service for leave and licence agreements.[^3] The stamp duty is 0.25% of the total rent for the term; a refundable deposit adds a little to it.[^4] There is a registration fee on top.

So in Pune or Mumbai, "we'll just do a notarised one" is not a favour to you.

### Tamil Nadu: registered with the Rent Authority

Tamil Nadu asks for tenancy agreements to be in writing and registered with its Rent Authority, which has its own portal.[^5]

## The deposit {#deposit}

This is where most of the money, and most of the arguments, sit.

**There is no national limit on deposits.** The Model Tenancy Act of 2021 says a deposit for a home should not be more than two months' rent.[^6] But it is a model: it applies only where a state has passed a law based on it. In mid-2022 the government listed four states that had revised their laws along its lines: Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Uttar Pradesh and Assam.[^7] In Tamil Nadu the figure is three months' rent, unless the agreement says otherwise.[^9]

Everywhere else, the deposit is what local custom and your bargaining make it. Bengaluru owners have long asked for up to ten months' rent, while two to three months is more usual in Delhi and Mumbai.[^10]

:::note About those "new rent rules" forwards
Every few months a message does the rounds saying a new national law now caps deposits at two months and fines owners. As of late 2026, no such central law has been notified.[^8] If someone quotes it at you, ask which state's Act they mean.
:::

### Getting it back

Where a state follows the Model Act, the deposit is to be refunded on the day you hand back the empty flat, less whatever you owe.[^6] Everywhere else, your agreement decides. So make sure it says two things:

- **When** the deposit comes back. "On the day of handing over the keys" is what you want. "Within a reasonable time" is what you don't.
- **What can come off it.** Unpaid rent and bills, and damage beyond ordinary wear, are fair. "One month's rent for painting", whatever state you leave the walls in, is a cost: know it before you sign.

:::tip The five-minute habit that saves deposits
On the day you move in, photograph every wall, floor and appliance and send the photos to the owner. On the day you leave, do it again. [The flat-visit checklist](post:flat-visit-checklist) has the full list.
:::

## Whose name is on it {#names}

Flatmates usually do one of two things, and each has a catch.

| | Everyone signs | One person signs |
|---|---|---|
| Who the owner can ask for rent | Any one of you, for the whole amount, unless the agreement says otherwise | Only the person who signed |
| If a flatmate stops paying | The rest of you may have to cover it | The signer has to cover it |
| If someone leaves | The agreement is changed, with the owner | If it is the signer, the others have no paper saying they may stay |
| Address proof and HRA | Each of you has the agreement | Only the signer does |

The first column comes from the Contract Act: when several people make a promise together, the other side can ordinarily ask any one of them to keep the whole of it.[^11] That sounds alarming and is usually still the better choice, because everyone has a right to be there and everyone has the paper.

If only one of you signs, get the owner's written okay (a message will do) naming who else lives in the flat. And agree between yourselves, in writing, who owes what: [how to split the rent and the deposit](post:split-rent-fairly).

> If your name isn't on anything, you are a guest who pays rent.

### When a flatmate is replaced

Tell the owner, and get the new name onto the agreement or into a written okay. Most agreements don't allow handing a room on without the owner's consent, and where the Model Act applies, subletting needs a written addition to the agreement.[^6]

## The clauses to read twice {#clauses}

:::checklist sign | Before you sign
# The basics
- The names are right: the owner's (and it matches their ID), and every flatmate's
- The flat's full address, and what comes with it: a list of furniture and appliances
- The term, the start date, and how it is renewed
# The money
- The rent, the day it is due, and how it is paid
- What is on top of the rent: maintenance, water, electricity, parking
- Any increase: how much, and from when
- The deposit: how much, when it comes back, and what can be deducted
# Leaving
- The notice period, for you and for the owner
- Any lock-in, and exactly what it costs to leave inside it
- The state the flat must be left in: painting, cleaning, repairs
# Living there
- Who pays for which repairs
- Rules on guests, pets and timings, including the society's
- When the owner may come in, and with how much warning
# The paper itself
- It is on stamp paper of the right value, or e-stamped
- It is registered where the state requires it
- I have my own signed copy
:::

A few of these deserve a word.

**Rent increases.** A rise during the term has to follow what the agreement says, which is the Model Act's rule too.[^6] If nothing is written, it shouldn't simply be announced half-way through. Many agreements write in an increase at renewal; look for the number.

**Lock-in and notice.** Notice is whatever the agreement says, so read that line; a month each way is common. A lock-in means that leaving before a set number of months costs you something. Know the number of months and the cost.

**Repairs.** The usual split: small things that wear out with use are yours, anything structural or to do with plumbing and wiring is the owner's. If the agreement is silent, ask and get the answer in a message.

## Police verification {#police}

In several cities the police ask owners to send them their tenants' details, usually through an order that is renewed from time to time. Mumbai has an online form for it,[^12] and so do the Pune area[^13] and Delhi.[^14] It is the owner's job, not yours, though you will be asked for your ID and a photo. It is normal; nothing has gone wrong.

## Two tax things to know exist {#tax}

- If you claim **HRA**, your employer will want rent receipts and, above a certain yearly rent, the owner's PAN. Ask payroll what they need before you sign, so you can ask the owner in time.
- If your own rent is high, the tax rules can require **you** to deduct tax (TDS) before paying the owner. The limits and rates have changed more than once in the last few years, so ask a chartered accountant, not a forward.

## The short of it {#short}

Read it. Get your name on it. Write down how the deposit comes back. And keep a signed copy somewhere other than the flat's group chat.
